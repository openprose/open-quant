# Illustrative selection-evidence policy

This is authored caller policy for the example, not an institutional strategy-acceptance rule. The subject and candidate population are fixed by the brief and receipt. Preserve candidate IDs, dataset roles, score units, seed/spawn identity and selected replicate. Account for all 64 selection scores and, when supplied, all 64 evaluation scores. Missing rows or mismatched identities limit the affected comparison.

Review each requested view separately. Identify how its candidate was chosen and which data support the reported score. A frozen-candidate evaluation must refer to the candidate selected earlier, with no choice based on evaluation scores. A newly chosen evaluation winner is a distinct selection result, even if its raw score improves or it happens to have the same identity.

For selected maxima, use the supplied independent-family threshold and assumptions for the 5% comparison. For independent evaluation of the frozen candidate, use the ordinary one-candidate threshold. An exceedance requires a statistic strictly above its applicable threshold; equality does not exceed it. Show the raw one-candidate tail for context without substituting it for the selected maximum's family comparison. Report met for an observed exceedance, not met for an observed non-exceedance, or unresolved when the needed score or binding is unavailable. These labels describe this statistical comparison, not proof of a real effect, economic usefulness, overall model acceptance or report fulfillment.

The missing-evaluation packet omits the evaluation vector and both evaluation-view observations. Their findings remain unresolved; do not infer zero scores, failure to perform evaluation, or a result from the selection vector. The expected frozen candidate remains identifiable from the selection record even though its evaluation observation is absent. The selection-sample finding remains available. The contradictory case adds authored producer claims without changing numerical evidence. Assess those claims against the selection process and the applicable probability interpretation.

Limit conclusions to the supplied synthetic model and one replicate. Identify real-world assumptions and evidence that are absent, including temporal dependence, candidate dependence, wider search history, market regimes, implementation costs and institutional approval. Do not invent an effective trial count or statistical correction when those assumptions are unavailable.
